# Searching for Primes: A Neural AlphaZero Approach to a Factoring Game 

Marcel Crasmaru September 22, 2026 

Abstract 

We study a one-player token game on an N × N board where tokens slide along diagonals or duplicate onto neighbouring ones to form a combinatorial rectangle R × S. A conserved integer weight W ′ and a strict monovariant guarantee O(N 2)-length solutions, placing the game in NP . We prove that reaching a final position factors this 2 N -bit W ′ into two N -bit factors 

V, M < 2N that encode the rectangle’s rows and columns. Consequently, solving the game for a balanced-semiprime target is equivalent to integer factoring. However, if the target rectangle is known, the solution reduces to two polynomial-time steps: a forced downward chip-flow and a 0 /1-polynomial factorisation leveraging Cohn’s theorem. The game’s entire difficulty is thus isolated to the initial number-theoretic split. Supplying the popcounts of the factors as a promise preserves this asymptotic hardness but bounds the target search space. We exploit this constrained space using a learned policy/value network and an AlphaZero-style Monte-Carlo tree search, empirically probing the limits of neural look-ahead on a factoring-equivalent environment. 

# 1 The game and the promise 

Write [ N ] = {0, 1, . . . , N − 1} and index the board by cells ( r, c ) ∈ [N ]2, with r the row (top to bottom) and c the column (left to right). A cell holds at most one token; a position is the set

Definition 1 (Moves) . Two moves are allowed. 

Slide. Replace an occupied ( r, c ) by any empty cell ( r′, c ′) on the same diagonal, r′ − c′ = r − c.

Duplicate. Remove an occupied cell on diagonal d and place two tokens on two distinct empty cells of diagonal d − 1 (so a duplicate at d requires nd−1 ≤ Ld−1 − 2). P is final if all non-empty columns have the same pattern: col c(P ) = col c′ (P ) whenever both are non-empty. A position is a (combinatorial) rectangle if 

P = R × S := {(r, c ) : r ∈ R, c ∈ S} for some R, S ⊆ [N ]. Every rectangle is final. Conversely a non-empty final position has one common pattern R

Problem 1 (Rectangularise, with promise) . Given a position P0 and integers p, q , under the promise that a final position with exactly p · q tokens is reachable from P0, output a sequence of moves transforming P0 into such a final position. Here p = |R| and q = |S| for the target rectangle R × S.

Remark 1 (Scope and motivation) . These choices simplify without softening the core. On a general (rectangular) board a rectangle target encodes an arbitrary two-factor split of the board weight, so the game is as hard as ordinary integer factoring; the square board and the balanced semiprime 

target (two N -bit primes) single out factoring’s canonical hard case, as in RSA. The popcounts 

p = |R| and q = |S| are then supplied as a promise . This does not make the instance easier—by Theorem 2 it stays as hard as splitting a balanced semiprime—but it fixes the dimensions of the target rectangle and thereby shrinks the search space a learning agent must explore. That is exactly our purpose: Sections 8–9 attack the promised problem with an AlphaZero-style [4] Monte-Carlo tree search guided by a learned policy/value network, and the popcount promise is the lever that turns target selection into a constrained search rather than an open one.

Lemma 4 (Canonical maximal start) . Among all positions of a fixed weight W , the “greedy-high” position n0—obtained by filling diagonals from the largest d downward until the weight W is exhausted—maximises Uk for every k. Consequently every rectangle R × S with W (R × S) = W

# 6 Main theorem 

Theorem 3. Under the p · q promise, if a final position is reachable then one is reachable within 

O(N 2) moves, and a move sequence can be produced in time Tfact (N ) + O(N 2), where Tfact is the time to split W ′ = W (P0)2 N −1 into its two factors below 2N —exactly splitting the balanced semiprime, and the only hard step; no sub-exponential bound for it is known. Given the split, both phases are polynomial: Step A by the forced downward flow, and Step B because the two 0/1 factors 

ρ, ˜σ are the base-2 expansions of the primes V, M , hence irreducible by Cohn’s theorem [5], so P

has exactly two irreducible factors that Z-factorisation recovers directly. (For a general, non-prime balanced split the regrouping of Proposition 2 instead costs N O(k).) Proof. Corollary 1 bounds the length. Compute n0 from P0 and W ′ = W (P0)2 N −1. Find V, M < 

2N with V · M = W ′, pop V = p, pop M = q (this is Tfact , the balanced-factoring step). Set 

R = bits( V ), S = {N − 1 − j : bit j of M } and let n∗ be their profile. Run Step A (Proposition 1) and then seat by slides (Proposition 2); both are O(N 2). Hardness is Theorem 2.

# 7 A worked example: factoring 143 

Take N = 4 and M = 143 = 11 · 13, with 11 = 1011 2 and 13 = 1101 2. Laying M on the board as the conserved weight W ′ = 143 by the greedy-high placement (Lemma 4) gives the seven-token start of Figure 2 (left). Two duplications (Step A) and three slides (Step B) reach the rectangle 

R × S with R = S = {0, 1, 3} (right): its occupied rows give V = 2 0 + 2 1 + 2 3 = 11 and its occupied columns give Msel = 2 3 + 2 2 + 2 0 = 13, so 143 = 11 · 13. The weight W ′ = 143 is unchanged on every board. The worked example was easy only because we already knew the factorisation. In general the one hard step is target selection —which rectangle R×S to aim for—and the popcount promise turns this into a bounded combinatorial search: the rows are a p-subset and the columns a q-subset of [ N ], and Step B gives a cheap, exact test of whether a candidate profile is a genuine factorisation. A short bounded action space (the duplications), a constrained target, and a verifiable 0 /1 terminal reward are exactly the ingredients an AlphaZero-style learner is built for: a policy network to propose moves and a value network to prune, wrapped in Monte-Carlo tree search over the promised targets. Sections 8–9 put this to the test—first cloning Step A with a network, then driving the search with it—and measure how far the promise’s bounded structure lets learning and look-ahead reach. # 8 A neural probe of the hardness: cloning Step A 

The reduction of the game to factoring is concrete enough to test empirically: one can train a neural network to imitate Step A move by move and ask whether it ever assembles a full solution. 1

It does not, and why it does not mirrors Theorem 2 exactly. 

Formulation. A state is the diagonal profile n ∈ Z2N −1 

> ≥0

(the coefficient vector of P ). An action is a duplication “at index i”, 1 ≤ i ≤ 2N − 2, sending ni → ni − 1 and ni−1 → ni−1 + 2; a distinguished stop action (the “ −1”) marks n = n∗. Because the weight P 

> i

ni2i = W ′ is conserved and determines n’s instance, the map n 7 → (next move) is a well-defined function—but computing it is exactly recovering the factorisation, so the learning target is factoring in supervised disguise. 

Data. For random N -bit primes p ≤ q we lay C = pq greedy-high (Lemma 4) to get n0, compute the rectangle target n∗, and replay the forced-flow duplication sequence of Proposition 1, emitting one (profile , move) pair per step and a terminal stop . One N = 64 instance yields ≈ 10 3 pairs; the canonical order p ≤ q fixes the target, hence the labels. 

Model and conditioning. The policy is a one-dimensional residual CNN (six convolutional blocks; a per-index 1 ×1 “move” head alongside a globally pooled stop head; ≈ 0.19M parameters), mapping features of shape (channels , 2N − 1) to 2 N logits. Three input regimes probe how much of the answer must be supplied: none (the profile only), pop (the profile plus the promised popcounts of 

p, q as constant channels), and target (the profile plus n∗ itself). Deeper variants add capacity and, with dilated kernels, receptive field; Figure 3 diagrams the largest instance—the depth-16 dilated 1.2M-parameter net used at N = 16—including the value head that the search of Section 9 adds. We adopt a one-dimensional dilated residual CNN rather than an attention-based Transformer because the board profile is an ordered 1D grid on which chip flow is strictly local ( d → d − 1); exponentially dilated convolutions attain a complete global receptive field across all 2 N −1 diagonals in ⌈log 2(2 N −1) ⌉ layers with minimal parameters. Because the barrier is cryptographic (Theorem 2), replacing local inductive biases with global self-attention cannot alter the asymptotic hardness.

(1) Counting 0/1-polynomial divisors. Step B must single out the divisors of P that them-selves have coefficients in {0, 1}. The crude estimate is the 2 k subset-products of the k irreducible factors of P , but genuine 0 /1 divisors are far rarer than arbitrary subset-products. We conjecture that every degree-D integer polynomial has at most DO(log D) divisors in {0, 1}[x]. A proof would let Step B enumerate them directly in quasi-polynomial time, making target selection—and hence the whole solver— unconditionally N O(log N ), with no hypothesis on the factor count k. The bound holds trivially when k = O(log N ) (then even 2 k is polynomial) and is false for unrestricted coeffi-cients; the gap between these is the open content, and by item (3) it is exactly what the worst-case boards exercise. 

(2) Exact complexity of the target-selection core. Recovering ( R, S ) from the profile n∗

alone (Proposition 2) is the factorisation of P = ρ ˜σ into two 0 /1 polynomials—a partial-digest / turnpike-type reconstruction of two sets. Under the substitution x 7 → 2 it becomes splitting the balanced semiprime M = ρ(2) ˜ σ(2), so the search problem is integer factoring in disguise. Its decision version—“does M have a factor in [2 N −1, 2N ) of popcount p?”—lies in NP ∩coNP : the prime 12 factorisation of M together with Pratt primality certificates [1] witnesses both the yes- and the no-answer. This is strong evidence that the core is not NP -complete, since that would force NP = coNP ;symmetrically, a polynomial-time algorithm for it would split balanced semiprimes classically. The core therefore sits squarely in the factoring-flavoured “intermediate” regime, and deciding between 

P and NP -completeness is tantamount to resolving the status of factoring itself—the precise sense in which its class is open. (For contrast, the classical turnpike problem—reconstruct points on a line from their multiset of pairwise differences—admits only a pseudo-polynomial backtracking algorithm [3] and has itself resisted classification for decades.)

(4) Quantum status. The game’s hardness core is balanced-semiprime splitting, and integer factoring is in BQP by Shor’s algorithm [2]—balancedness neither helps nor hinders Shor, which factors any composite in polynomial time. Hence target selection is in BQP : a quantum machine factors M = pq in time poly( N ) and, composed with the classical polynomial Steps A and B, outputs the full O(N 2) move sequence in quantum polynomial time. The decision problem “is a final position of at most p·q tokens reachable?” is therefore in BQP , and the game is not a candidate for post-quantum hardness: its apparent classical intractability is exactly as quantum-fragile as the factoring problem it encodes.

The geometry is easy; the target is hard. The full-or-R variant exposes a clean decoupling in the game’s complexity. Complementing the winning condition radically alters the number-theoretic target—from W ′ to A2 − W ′—while leaving the geometric mechanics (Steps A and B) completely unchanged. The physical constraints of the board are therefore computationally easy: whatever the target number, duplications and slides reach it in polynomial time once it is known . The intractable core of the token game is purely the algebraic search for that target. More broadly, the operation # recasts the board as a visual calculator for polynomial arithmetic. One column pattern makes W ′ a single product V · M (Section 3); k patterns make it a sum of k

products (Remark 4); the full-or-R condition realises the De Morgan dual A2 − (A − U )( A − W ). In every case the board’s rules are evaluating a polynomial , and the “gameplay”—driving tokens to a final position—is the search for its factorisation: for the roots that split the target over the promised {0, 1} alphabet.
